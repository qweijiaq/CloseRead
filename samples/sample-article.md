# The Quiet Cost of Cheap Attention

Every morning, millions of people wake up and reach for a small rectangle of glass. Before they have spoken to another person, they have already given a piece of their attention to a company they will never meet. The exchange feels free. It is not.

Attention has become one of the defining commodities of the modern economy. Unlike oil or wheat, it cannot be stored, shipped, or saved for later. It is scarce, personal, and perishable. The platforms that capture it rarely sell it directly; they sell access to the people whose attention they have collected. Advertisers pay for the chance to interrupt a moment that might otherwise have belonged to the reader.

The economics are simple; the consequences are not. When a resource is abundant, companies compete on quality. When attention is scarce, they compete on capture. The result is a landscape designed to produce compulsion rather than satisfaction: endless feeds, autoplay, notifications, streaks, and small rewards delivered at unpredictable intervals. Each feature is rational for the platform. Together, they can undermine a person's ability to sustain deliberate thought.

Fragmentation is the hidden tax. A task that once required an hour of quiet now arrives in a dozen pieces, each one separated by a notification, a headline, or a short video. The mind adapts to the rhythm of interruption, and the adaptation is almost invisible. People do not notice that they have stopped reading long sentences; they simply begin to avoid them. The loss is not only time. It is the capacity to follow an argument from beginning to end.

The word "friction" has become a villain in product design. A little friction, however, is what turns an impulse into a decision. Making it slightly harder to open an app, or slightly easier to close it, can restore a measure of autonomy. Some companies have begun to sell subscription products precisely because they do not need to interrupt their users. Their incentive is to make the experience durable rather than addictive.

Critics of this view sometimes argue that people should simply pay attention better. But attention is not a muscle that grows through scolding. It is a skill shaped by the environment. Children who grow up in a room full of interruptions learn a different relationship to silence than children who do not. Adults who spend eight hours a day responding to alerts become good at responding and worse at staying. The damage is gradual, which is why it is easy to dismiss.

This is not an argument that attention is a zero-sum game. A well-made documentary, a difficult novel, or a thoughtful conversation can leave a person more alert than before. The question is who gets to choose the direction of that alertness. When every available surface is optimized for extraction, the burden of curation falls on the individual. That is a heavy job, and most people are already tired.

The most promising response may be neither abstinence nor willpower but design. Tools that protect a block of time, screens that stay quiet until asked, and products that charge for use instead of attention all point in the same direction. They treat attention as something to be spent wisely, not something to be harvested.

The cost of cheap attention is not always visible on a bill. It appears in the book you did not finish, the conversation you did not hear, and the idea that never had room to develop. Reclaiming that space is not a matter of discipline alone. It is a matter of choosing, again and again, what deserves to be noticed.
